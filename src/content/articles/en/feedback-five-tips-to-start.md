## 1. Meet face to face

- **In person** — the best option, because it also allows nonverbal communication. That makes it easier to understand each other and to clear up any misunderstandings quickly.
- **On video** — we usually work remotely, so meeting in person is not always possible. Zoom is a good alternative in that case, but remember: turn on your camera!
- **By voice** — not everyone will want to, or be able to, turn on a camera. Then you're left with audio. You lose some nonverbal communication, but it's still a better option than the last one on this list. And even if the other person leaves their camera off, it's good if at least you turn yours on while giving feedback.
- **On Slack** — it seems like the easiest and quickest option. It only seems that way, because a whole lot of things can go wrong:
  - without nonverbal cues, it's easy to **misinterpret** a message and give it **the wrong tone**
  - giving feedback this way, you often get **no sign that you've been understood correctly**
  - there's no room to clarify the context, add something, or explain
  - and if someone does ask about those things, you end up **spending time typing messages** on Slack, when face to face you'd have settled it in a few minutes

## 2. Take care of the circumstances (time, space, and mood)

### Make sure you both have time

- It's best to give feedback right after something happens. If you go to someone and say, “Last Thursday, during refinement, there was this thing and I didn't quite get what you meant,” you can't count on them remembering the details of that meeting well enough to respond. Still, it's worth taking a few minutes' break before giving feedback.
- What if you're in a meeting and want to give someone feedback? It's usually not a good idea to do it in front of everyone. Not everybody is comfortable receiving feedback in front of others. Even positive feedback.
- If the person you want to give feedback to is clearly busy, in a hurry, or about to finish work, try to arrange a conversation at the next convenient time. That could be in fifteen minutes or even the next day. It isn't ideal, but if you give feedback when someone has no time for it, you simply won't be heard.

<figure class="article-figure"><img src="/images/feedback/do-you-have-a-moment-for-feedback.jpg" width="1280" height="720" alt="Meme of Darth Vader asking whether you have a moment for feedback" loading="lazy" /></figure>

### Find the right place

- It's not a good idea to give feedback in a room full of people. Make sure you can talk privately.
- You need a feel for the right moment and place. Don't do it, for example, when someone is about to go into the bathroom, even if the corridor is empty.
- That doesn't mean you have to book a meeting room. Just try to make sure nobody interrupts you.

### Keep it calm

- If you can see that someone is under strong emotions, wait a few minutes and give them time to cool down.
- If you can see that someone feels unwell, is tired, or is in a bad mood, you can arrange to talk the next day.
- Apply both of these points to yourself, too. Giving feedback in a bad mood can have the opposite effect to what you intended.

## 3. Ask whether they're ready for feedback

Before giving anyone feedback, you should first make sure the person is open to it and will listen to you. That's why it's always worth starting with a question like:

> I'd like to give you some feedback. Can we talk?

<figure class="article-figure"><img src="/images/feedback/are-you-ready-for-some-more-constructive-feedback.jpg" width="460" height="380" alt="Meme asking Are you ready for some more constructive feedback?" loading="lazy" /></figure>

A question like this lets the other person know that feedback is coming, so they're ready for it in a way. They'll listen to you deliberately and be able to draw their own conclusions. By checking first, you also lower the chance of a misunderstanding.

## 4. Focus on facts

Once you're giving feedback, focus on not judging the other person. Don't talk about how, in your view, they did something wrong. Describe the situation and the behavior, and point to the effect of that behavior.

<aside class="example example-good" aria-label="Example describing the situation"><span class="example-mark" aria-hidden="true">✓</span><p>Yesterday, before refinement, we talked about having to add extra requirements to task XY-555. In refinement we discussed that task and you didn't mention it at all. Because of that, we may have estimated the task incorrectly.</p></aside>

<aside class="example example-bad" aria-label="Example judging the person"><span class="example-mark" aria-hidden="true">×</span><p>Yesterday, before refinement, we talked about having to add extra requirements to task XY-555. You're misleading the team and causing unnecessary confusion instead of saying things plainly.</p></aside>

## 5. Paraphrase

Paraphrasing, putting what you heard into your own words, is a great technique for reaching mutual understanding. It doesn't just help you understand each other; it also shows that you really heard what the other person had to say. Paraphrasing is a way to get to the other person's need. How does that work with feedback?

If you're giving feedback about something someone said, you can start by paraphrasing it:

<aside class="example example-good" aria-label="Conversation with paraphrasing"><span class="example-mark" aria-hidden="true">✓</span><div>
<p>— In the meeting, you said these tasks make no sense. Does that mean they're unclear to you?</p>
<p>— No, I mean they make no sense. They won't be any use to the business.</p>
<p>— So you think they have no value from the user's point of view, right?</p>
<p>— Well, yes. The user won't get anything out of them.</p>
<p>— Does that mean there may be no need to do them?</p>
<p>— Exactly.</p>
<p>— Could you put together arguments for dropping these tasks?</p>
<p>— Sure.</p>
</div></aside>

Notice that here the feedback isn't given directly, but through paraphrasing questions and a request. What will the effect be? Someone who simply criticized the tasks in one meeting will bring arguments to the next one, and that meeting may turn out to be much more productive.

<aside class="example example-bad" aria-label="Conversation without paraphrasing"><span class="example-mark" aria-hidden="true">×</span><div>
<p>— In the meeting, you said these tasks make no sense. Does that mean they're unclear to you?</p>
<p>— No, I mean they make no sense. They won't be any use to the business.</p>
<p>— But why don't they make sense?</p>
<p>— Because there's no point wasting time on something useless.</p>
<p>— Okay, but why do you think these tasks won't add anything?</p>
<p>— Because the user won't use it.</p>
<p>…</p>
</div></aside>

When we don't paraphrase, it's harder to get to the heart of the matter, and frustration grows because both sides feel misunderstood.

> If you receive feedback, paraphrase it too, to make sure you understood it correctly.
